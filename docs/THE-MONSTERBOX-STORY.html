First — what's a “commit”? (a 30-second primer)

A commit is a saved snapshot of the code at one moment — like hitting “save” on the entire project, with a short note attached describing what just changed. Developers commit constantly (often dozens of times a day).

Why they do it: every commit is a checkpoint they can rewind to, so a mistake is never fatal — you roll back to the last good snapshot. It's also how a team works on the same code without overwriting each other, and a running history of who changed what, when, and why.

Every commit carries a one-line message. Those messages are what this document reads — all 2,469 of them — to reconstruct how the project, and the AI building it, evolved.

First — what is MonsterBox? A web app that runs living Halloween creatures on a $75 computer

It's Halloween night in a garage in Coralville, Iowa. A life-sized vampire — Orlok — turns his head to track a kid coming up the driveway. His eyes glow; he leans in, jaw moving in time, and speaks: not a recording, but a live, AI-generated reply to whatever the kid just said. A video flickers across a tombstone, lights pulse with the audio, a second creature stirs. Nobody is backstage pulling levers. It's all MonsterBox — an animatronic control platform on a Raspberry Pi 4B wired to servos, motors, lights, sensors, a camera, a speaker, and a mic.

What you can do with it on Halloween today:

🎬 Animation StudioDrag servo moves, motors, lights, audio, and pauses onto a timeline; loop scenes all night or fire them on a sensor
🗣️ Talk & listenBuilt-in text-to-speech gives each character a voice; speech-to-text lets it hear a guest
🤖 Converse with AIAn askAI step sends what a guest said to an LLM and speaks the reply back — the monster improvises, jaw auto-synced
👁️ Track peopleUSB camera + OpenCV motion detection drives head-tracking so a character's gaze follows movement
📺 Project videoThe Goblin subsystem plays synchronized video/playlists on external displays
💡 Move & light it allLEDs, PWM servos (PCA9685), motors, linear actuators — live from the dashboard or sequenced in a scene

In short: video, lights, motion, voice, hearing, and AI conversation — all coordinated from one web app.

Five creatures, different hardware and voices and personalities, on one engine — which is exactly why "character independence" stopped being a nicety and became the architecture.

It didn't start anywhere near here. The first version (Aug 2024) was one demon named Baphomet, a couple hundred lines of hand-written Node.js, three flat JSON files, and commit messages like fff and embracesuck typed at midnight before the holiday. No AI conversation, no video, no head-tracking, no multi-character support, no tests — one person copy-pasting code and hoping it held for one night. Getting from there to here took two years, and the road runs exactly parallel to how AI coding tools evolved in real time — from a clever autocomplete that broke the lights, to a swarm of agents that over-built everything overnight, to a governed collaborator that finally earned trust. That's the story below; the lessons for anyone building with AI in 2026 are at the end.

The real story isn't which model won — it's how the way of using them climbed seven rungs:

1 · Complete the lineClaude 3.5 via Claude Dev (2024) — describe a function, paste the result, wire it up by hand. No view of the system, so it broke the LEDs.
2 · Pick a model for a jobGemini (Oct 2024) for a mechanical refactor (tearing out AJAX) — the first "right tool for this task" move.
3 · Own the taskClaude + GPT + Gemini together behind Cursor/Windsurf/Roo/Augment + TaskMaster over MCP (2025) — models became interchangeable labor you dispatched; leverage moved to the harness.
3.5 · Become the productOpenAI GPT inside ChatterPi (2025) — the model stopped building the product and became a feature of it.
4 · Run unsupervisedGPT-5 overnight (Sept 2025) — maximum autonomy, zero restraint → a web service for every body part.
5 · Operate under governanceClaude Opus via Claude Code (early 2026) — a senior agent trusted because it works inside a constitution, schemas, and a gate it can't commit past.
6 · Be briefed, not instructedClaude Fable 5 / 5.1 + Opus 5 (late 2026) — the constitution stays; the step-by-step instructions get deleted, because "the scaffolding becomes the ceiling." You supply the constraint, the verification, and the context; the model picks the method.
7 · Be accountable to another agentA foreman: session supervising a worker session (Aug 21 2026) — both committing to the same repo, one of them publishing a retraction of its own order because the worker was right to refuse it.

Same three model families throughout — the change was the interaction pattern maturing faster than the guardrails each new rung required. The vendor mattered less than the harness; the harness mattered less than whether a human had yet encoded the judgment the model still lacked.

3c · The Microservices Debacle Sept 2025 — GPT-5, too young, given the keys overnight, turned every body part into a web service

Given broad autonomy, GPT-5 decided everything should be a web service. Watch it metastasize:

Websockets 1.0 → Hardware Sockets 1.0 → WebSocket Centralized Management → Phase 1 Websockets 2.0 → WS 2.1 → Websockets 3.0 — fully individual RPI4bs → MASSIVE Sockets Improvement for PArts → WORKING Servos and Servo Services → Services for ALL!

By Services for ALL! (Sat Sept 6, 19:51) every servo, jaw, and eye had its own service and port — 110+ service/socket files. The session ground on overnight: Full Build (21:29) → almost there… (22:05) → Big changes across (01:24) → a single exhausted good (01:33). Two days later: fucking sockers, then mess.

The reset was brutal and lossy: first commit - MonsterBox4.0 → MONSTERBOX 4.0 CONVERSION - Single Node No Services — one commit, 770 files changed, 13,101 lines deleted. The microservice empire was torn out; the single-node design finally held (after one relapse: Final AI stretch - move BACK TO WEBSOCKETS). Features were lost in the reset. This is why CLAUDE.md now bans new transport layers in capital letters — that rule is a scar from this one night.

4 · Bloat, then Discipline Tracked-file count at each release tag — AI generation inflated the codebase; stabilization shrank it

By v5.3.0 the repository had ballooned to 1,889 tracked files (including vendored node_modules and a flood of AI-generated tests and scripts). The 2026 stabilization effort cut it nearly in half — to 908 — while making the surviving code provably character-independent. Growth stopped being the goal; governed simplicity became it.

Tracked-file counts swing partly with vendoring decisions (node_modules was committed, then untracked). The direction — explosive growth under AI generation, then deliberate reduction under governance — is the real signal.

Every guardrail built in 2026 assumed one machine. Prove a fix landed by grepping the node for the actual symbol." On one box, "what version is this" is answered by a string. On a fleet, the string is a rumour.

August 2026 — 342 commits, and for the first time a big month that isn't panic. The second-busiest month in the project's history, behind only Oct 2024 (601) and ahead of Oct 2025 (332). 7 · When the Commit Message Became a Sentence The log stopped describing diffs and started recording findings — 13 → 89 chars

Lift twenty consecutive subjects out of the late-2026 log and they read as English sentences about the world:

the five reasons Mina's controls did nothing, and said nothing
the fleet was never offline — six endless streams ate the browser's connection pool
the cameras were fine; the panels lied about them
losing a measured window is no longer a one-way door
a version string is not proof of a deploy · a speed cap that forgets is not a safety cap
the ratchet caught its own bug-hunter

None of them describe the change — they describe what turned out to be the case. The diff is in the diff; the subject line is now reserved for the conclusion that made the diff correct. An extraordinary number are negations of a believed fact ("was never broken", "was never quiet", "was never offline", "the cameras were fine") — which names the project's true defect class: most of what looked like broken hardware was software reporting a state it had not checked. And they are honest about their own limits: the part probe failed — record that, and correct the checklist it produced, NEEDS FINISHER VERIFICATION, what shipped tonight, and what is still unverified.

The sentiment instrument finally breaks — and the failure is the finding. Profanity: 9 in Oct'24 · 3 in Jun'25 · 2 in Sep'25 · 2 in Oct'25 · zero every month since. But a naïve frustration scan now fires twelve times in Aug'26, and every hit is a false positive: Mina was never broken, the control bar was never broken. In 2024, broke meant I am suffering. In 2026, broken is a hypothesis being ruled out. The cheap instrument that told you the truth for two years eventually starts lying — and the tell is that its errors all point the same way.

Quieter machinery from the same period: a post-commit hook that runs every tenth commit and prints a fresh log review (a reminder, not a gate — deliberately too cheap to resent); a 3,204-line KNOWN-BUGS.md organised per animatronic and per session, where items are struck through "only with proof (two clean boots, a verified probe)"; and ratchets that only tighten — the character-independence allowlist 73 → 39 → 23, design-system violations 1,429 → 211 → 93. On Aug 22 a line goes by that closes a loop opened in 2024: first green CI Test Suite run in repo history (run 32544233328). Two years, 2,400 commits. The commit does not celebrate; it records the run number.

8 · The Scaffolding Was the Ceiling Aug–Sep 2026 — a new model arrives, and half the first edition's thesis turns out to be wrong

The first edition ended on a clean thesis: capability is abundant, governance is the constraint, so write the constitution down. Eleven months inside that constitution produced a correction, recorded in a skill file that is not documentation of a product but a field manual written by someone who paid for the lessons:

Prompts and skills written for older models are too prescriptive for Fable 5 and measurably reduce its output quality. Step-by-step scaffolding, "CRITICAL: you MUST", enumerated prohibitions, forced progress cadences — these were mitigations for models that under-followed instructions. Fable follows them literally and the scaffolding becomes the ceiling.

State the goal, the constraints, and how to verify. Let it choose the method. When migrating an older prompt, the first experiment is deleting the scaffolding, not rewriting it.

But the gate stayed. What got deleted was not the constraints — it was the method instructions:

KEPT, and tightenedConstraints (never a new transport layer; every hardware call passes characterId; the allowlist may only shrink) · Verification (a deploy is proven by grep on the node; audio by ear-check; reject simulated:true) · Context (the incident behind each risky part) · The gate
DELETED, as a quality capProcedure ("first X, then Y, then Z") · Emphasis ("CRITICAL", "you MUST") · Enumerated prohibitions standing in for one stated reason · Forced cadences

The left column is what must be true and how you'll know. The right column is how to do it. Governance moved off the right and onto the left, and quality went up.

The one instruction block worth more than all the rest — and it is the whole project in a paragraph:

Ground every progress claim in a tool result. This is the single highest-value block for MonsterBox, because the repo's recurring defect class is software reporting the number it was given rather than the one it used.

The bug in the animatronics — a success: true that means an API returned, not that a servo moved — and the bug in the AI — a progress report that means text was generated, not that work landed — are the same bug. The ear-check and "audit each claim against a tool result" are one idea applied twice.

Choosing a model the way you'd choose a person. Assignments are by job shape, with reasons: fable for long-horizon and ambiguous work; opus for deep reasoning with real-world blast radius; sonnet where judgment is classification and runs often; haiku only where a wrong answer is cheaply caught. And then, in bold, the rule that could only come from this particular garage:

Do not put a hardware-driving agent on Fable. Fable is documented to take unrequested-but-adjacent actions, and on this fleet the adjacent action is commanding a servo on a rail that has already blown a fuse. That is a deliberate exception, not an oversight.

The most mature sentence about AI in the whole two-year history. Not "this model is unsafe" — but here is a documented behavioural tendency, here is its specific physical consequence in my specific yard, therefore here is a narrow exception, and I am recording that it is intentional so nobody 'fixes' it later. It is the exact inverse of the GPT-5 failure: that model wasn't given too much power, it was given power whose blast radius nobody had matched to the model's temperament.

In 2025 the constraint on running an agent swarm was how many API keys you had. In 2026, on this project, it is how many cores are in the animatronic's head: min(16, cores − 2) = 2 agents at a time on a 4-core Pi. Wide adversarial fan-outs belong in a cloud session. And: "size an agent by what it can finish, not by what is logically one task" — the journal only records a result when an agent returns, so a monolith that dies at event 161 has journaled nothing and a resume replays nothing.

And verification gets its own agent. "A separate verifier agent that never saw the build reliably outperforms asking the builder to self-critique. On this repo the verification bar is physical, not textual." A builder checking its own work is checking its own reasoning — and its reasoning is the thing that was wrong. A fresh verifier checks the artifact. On a machine with servos in it, the artifact of last resort is not a passing test. It is a microphone, in a garage, hearing a vampire.

Lessons for Anyone Building With AI in 2026 Fourteen scars from 2,469 commits, each paid for in a Fucked Halloween or a silent animatronic

1 · Capability before judgmentThe newest, most powerful models did the most spectacular damage — not because they were dumb, but because they had no instinct for restraint. Knowing what not to build is your job, not the model's.
2 · Autonomy is a loanThe overnight "let it run" session felt like a windfall and cost a 770-file, 13,000-line teardown. Give an agent rope only as fast as you build the checks that catch it.
3 · Safety in the systemNothing improved reliability until guardrails became automated and unskippable — schemas, a resolver, a contract suite, a pre-deploy gate. Reviewing by eye doesn't scale; a gate the AI can't commit past does.
4 · Write the constitution downA CLAUDE.md that says "DO NOT introduce new transport layers" turns a hard-won scar into a rule honored on every future task. Encode decisions as machine-readable constraints, not tribal knowledge.
5 · Beware the load-bearing defaultSo much was hardcoded to Orlok that "character independence" became a multi-month crusade. AI cements whatever it sees first into an assumption everywhere. Design for the general case early.
6 · Smaller is a featureAI generation inflated the repo to 1,889 files; the healthiest era cut it nearly in half. More code is not more progress — the most mature commits here removed things.
7 · Commit messages are an instrumentThe clearest signal of how the work was really going — tooling, discipline, even mood — was hiding in the log the whole time. Write them like they'll be read later.
8 · The human's job changed, not shrankOver two years the human wrote less code and became more essential. AI got better at the how; the whether and the why stayed human.
9 · The green light is the bugThe dominant defect class here is not broken hardware — it is software reporting a state it never checked. Ask of every status: what would this look like if it were wrong? If the answer is "identical," you don't have a status, you have a decoration.
10 · Verify at the physical layersuccess: true proves an API call; the ear-check proves sound in the air. A version endpoint proves a string; grepping the node proves a deploy. Pick the cheapest measurement that lives outside the thing making the claim — then automate it, because a check that needs a human standing in the yard gets skipped on the night it matters.
11 · Record what you have not provenThe best artifact in the repo is a config whose entries carry their own evidence, including "MEASURED, NOT YET EAR-VERIFIED." AI collaborators are relentlessly willing to round uncertainty to done; a record that carries it at full strength is the cheapest correction available.
12 · Scaffolding expiresEvery "CRITICAL: you MUST" was a mitigation for a model that under-followed instructions. Give it to a model that follows literally and you've capped it at your own imagination. When you upgrade models, the first experiment is deleting your scaffolding, not rewriting it. Keep the constraints, the verification and the context; hand back the method.
13 · Match blast radius to the model"Do not put a hardware-driving agent on Fable… that is a deliberate exception, not an oversight." Model selection is a safety control, not a performance tweak — and an exception that isn't labelled deliberate will be optimised away by whoever finds it next.
14 · A rule is a proxy for a reasonWhen the operator deleted every per-part safety limit — "we don't need them ever again" — the right move was neither to argue nor to silently comply: remove the enforcement, record the ruling verbatim with its date, and promote the incidents to briefing material. Governance that can only add constraints isn't governance; it's accretion.
First edition, and still true of the era it described: AI in 2026 is powerful enough to build almost anything and wise enough to build almost nothing.
Second edition: AI in late 2026 will do almost anything you ask and is better than you at choosing how. The leverage is no longer in telling it what to do — and no longer only in the guardrails. It is in being the one who decides what would count as proof, and then building the instrument that settles it. That's not a limitation to wait out. It's the job.

Appendix · How This Document Was Written (Together) The recursive twist: a story about building with AI, built with AI

No human reads 2,020 commit messages, tallies the swear words by month, cross-references config directories against their deletion dates, and traces one character's name through hundreds of JSON revisions. The data was always there, in the open, in the repository — but unreadable at that scale by the person who created it.

The human brought the memory and the meaning — knew there was a Baphomet, an overnight session gone wrong, "an AI too young." Those threads came from having lived it, not from the data.
The AI brought the reading and the recall — unshallowed the history, ran dozens of queries across all 2,020 commits, found the 01:33 timestamp and the verbatim good, measured the 13→70 climb.

Then it became a conversation: the human recognized some findings, corrected others ("it wasn't always MonsterBox," "I lost features in the reset"), and pointed at the next thread; the AI went back to the data and returned with evidence. Round after round, that built this document.

And the document you're reading is the product of that loop, still running. It didn't arrive whole — it accreted, one prompt at a time. The first pass was a plain timeline. Then: more narrative, the sentiments in the commits, the tools used → the AI mined the swear-word counts and tool lifecycles. Was there an older Baphomet repo? → it found the "Scary Pete" root commit and corrected the myth. The overnight microservice disaster → it pulled the 01:33 timestamps that proved it. Look into the characters → Orlok, Mina, and Sir Dragomir walked out of the scene files. The real LLM story → it mapped the five-rung climb from "complete the line" to "operate under governance." I hate Markdown — give me a publication PDF that keeps the visuals → it wrote a renderer that redraws the charts natively. The pagination sucks, add a legend, explain what a commit is up front → and that's why this page is laid out the way it is. Even this paragraph exists because the human asked the AI to write down how the two of them were writing it.

The same partnership that ships animatronic code under a pre-deploy gate also wrote its own origin story. The human supplies the memory, the judgment, and the question; the AI supplies the tireless reading; and the truth lives in the commits — invisible to one, inaccessible to the other — until the two work together.
Postscript · how the second edition was written

The prompt got shorter, not longer. The whole brief was, in substance: here is the first edition, here is the new codebase and the originator repo, expand it with new chapters, use the commit notes and the prompts to inform the narrative. No outline, no required sections, no chapter count. Per section 8, that is now the correct shape of an instruction. The first edition accreted across many rounds of "the pagination sucks, add a legend." This one was assembled from a paragraph.

The prompts became primary sources. In April the evidence was commits, configs and file counts — the artifacts of building. The instructions to the AI became the most honest description of the system — which happened because a prompt has to be true to work.

And the corrections ran in both directions. The first edition's human corrected the AI's myths. This one had to correct the first edition: its Baphomet has a namesake that predates it by six months and is a frozen fork of AutoGPT; its sentiment instrument now returns twelve false positives a month; and its closing thesis survives only as half of a distinction it did not yet know it needed to draw. The right response is the one the project itself arrived at — date it, source it, say which parts are measured and which are merely believed, and expect the next edition to catch something this one got wrong.
